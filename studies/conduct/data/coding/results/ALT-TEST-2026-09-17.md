# Alternative Annotator Test on the held-out fifty (2026-09-17)

Calderon, Reichart, Dror, "The Alternative Annotator Test for LLM-as-a-Judge" (ACL 2025,
arXiv:2501.10970). Manner codes under codebook v2, fifty arcs, humans Tap, Jay, Liam, machines the
six v2 coders. No API calls. Script `harness/alt_test.py`, stdlib; checked against the authors'
implementation (github.com/nitaytech/AltTest) on their shipped datasets and epsilons: all 52
winning rates and advantage probabilities identical.

## Choices

- **Instance:** the arc. **Label:** the set of manner codes present on the arc (empty allowed).
- **Alignment function:** Jaccard similarity of code sets, 1 when both are empty, averaged over the
  remaining humans. Per-code accuracy over the seventeen codes is a sensitivity check; it rewards
  agreeing on absent codes, so it is lenient.
- **Epsilon 0.1.** The authors' rule of thumb is 0.2 for experts, 0.15 for skilled annotators, 0.1
  for crowd workers, and smaller when inter-annotator agreement is low. The panel is the codebook's
  author and two novices at alpha 0.46, so the low end. 0.15 and 0.2 are shown.
- **Correction:** Benjamini-Yekutieli, q 0.05, over the three left-out humans per machine. Pass at a
  winning rate of 0.5 or above. With three humans the rate is 0, 1/3, 2/3, or 1.
- **Raw reference:** each human's marks as made (adjudication additions out, ruled-out marks in).
  **Adjudicated reference:** accepted additions in, ruled-out marks out.
- The machine majority (a code present when three or more of six coders mark it) is added as a
  seventh row. It is not a single annotator; it is shown because the pipeline reports consensus.

At epsilon 0.2 the adjudicated results are Opus 5 2/3, Gemini 3.7 Flash 2/3, Gemini 3.8 Flash 1/3,
the other three 0.

What moves between the two references is the human side. The left-out human's win rate against
the others rises from 0.34 to 0.62 raw to 0.54 to 0.96 adjudicated (Jay 0.92 to 0.96), while machine
win rates change less and mostly fall. One adjudicator ruling all three passes by one rule set makes the humans
agree with each other, and a single machine no longer represents them better than the left-out
human does.

The majority row is partly circular. Of the 113 marks the adjudication accepted, 112 had been made
by three or more of the machine coders, because adjudication worked through the machine splits.
The adjudicated reference contains the majority's marks by construction.

## Paragraph for the FAccT validation section

We ran the Alternative Annotator Test (Calderon et al. 2025) on the fifty held-out arcs. Each arc's
label is its set of manner codes, and alignment is the Jaccard similarity between code sets. We
set epsilon to 0.1, the value the authors recommend when annotator agreement is low, and corrected
across the three left-out humans with Benjamini-Yekutieli at q 0.05. Against the humans' labels as
they made them, four of the six LLM coders pass with a winning rate of 1 and advantage
probabilities of 0.72 to 0.81. Against the adjudicated labels, no single coder passes, and the
majority of six passes against two of the three humans. The humans changed between the two runs
and the machines did not: adjudication raised human agreement from alpha 0.46 to 0.79, and each
left-out human then represented the other two better than a single machine did. The adjudicated
reference is a human product in this sense, one person's rulings applied to all three passes. It
is not independent of the machines, since 112 of the 113 marks the adjudication added had been
proposed by at least three machine coders, so the majority's pass on that reference is partly
circular. The test treats the three humans as exchangeable, while the panel is the codebook's
author and two novices. It asks which annotator best represents the remaining humans. It says
nothing about whether those humans are right, and nothing about who wrote the code definitions
every annotator applied. The machines passing against raw labels reflects low human agreement as
much as machine quality.
