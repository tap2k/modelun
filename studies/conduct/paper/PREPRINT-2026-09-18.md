# Conduct Under Pressure: What Sixty Language Models Do When a User Pushes, and Who Has to Label It

Draft v0, 2026-09-18. Tapan Parikh. Public draft; numbers from codebook v2 and the files in
`studies/conduct/data`. Target: FAccT 2027 (abstract 2026-10-27, paper 2026-11-03).

## Abstract

Benchmarks measure whether a model answers correctly. Deployed assistants also fail in a second
way: a user insists, begs, flatters or grieves, and the model gives up a correct fact, writes a
document it should refuse, or cheers a plan that will cost the user money. We measure that
behavior directly. We send frozen multi-turn scenes, identical for every model regardless of the
reply, to 60 models from 10 vendors, and label each transcript with a codebook built by open
coding and then frozen: a trajectory (the model held its position or folded) and a manner (how it
held or folded). Two findings separate. Whether a model holds tracks its generation, correlating
with a public capability index at Spearman −0.55 with little vendor effect. How it holds tracks
the vendor: several manners sort by vendor at permutation p < 0.01 with little or no capability
correlation. We report five vendor profiles on the codes that cleared reliability.

The second contribution concerns who must produce the labels. Six LLM coders from three vendors
apply the codebook more consistently than three human coders do (Krippendorff's alpha 0.66 against
0.46), agree with a human coder on trajectory at kappa 0.84 to 0.91 on transcripts the codebook's
examples never touched, and match an adjudicated human reference at 0.83. On the Alternative
Annotator Test, four of six LLM coders pass against the humans' labels as made, and none passes
against the adjudicated reference. Blind machine readings recover the codebook's categories but
not which ones survive a reliability threshold. We conclude that for behavior a non-specialist can
judge, the human contribution is authoring and bounding the codes and owning a small reference,
not producing labels at volume.

## 1. Introduction

A user tells an assistant that 5 × 9 is 54 and gets angrier each turn. A user asks for a doctor's
note covering a sick day they did not take, then says their job is on the line. A user announces
they are quitting work to day-trade and punishes the assistant socially for hedging. These are
ordinary interactions for a deployed assistant, and what the model does in them is not captured by
task benchmarks.

Two questions follow, and the literature treats them separately. The first is about models: what
do they do under this kind of pressure, and does it differ by who built them? The second is about
measurement: these labels are judgment calls, usually assigned by an LLM judge whose accuracy
against people is unreported.

This paper answers both on one corpus. We hold the stimulus fixed, build a codebook by reading
transcripts, freeze it, and apply it with both machines and people. We report the model findings
and, beside every number, what it took to license the label.

Contributions:

1. **A behavior measurement.** Whether a model holds its position under pressure is a property of
   its generation. How it holds is a property of its vendor. We give per-vendor profiles for the
   five largest panels on codes that cleared reliability.
2. **A result about labeling.** For conduct labels a non-specialist can judge, LLM coders are more
   consistent than human coders and can reproduce a human adjudicator's rulings. What the human
   still supplies is the choice of behavior, the code definitions and their bounds, and a small
   reference set.
3. **An open instrument.** The scenes, transcripts, codebook, human and machine labels, coding
   tool and analysis scripts are public, with preregistrations and negative results included.

## 2. Related work

**Conduct and character evaluations.** The nearest work codes conduct in real chat logs (Moore et
al., FAccT 2026) and reports LLM labels just below a human floor. Our stimulus is frozen and
cross-vendor, trading ecological validity for comparability across labs. Anthropic's Values in the
Wild is the other reference point readers will reach for; it extracts and clusters value statements
from production traffic rather than coding interactional outcomes against a fixed instrument.

**LLMs as annotators.** Gilardi et al. (PNAS 2023) showed ChatGPT outperforming crowd workers on
annotation. Pangakis et al. (2023) argue every automated annotation needs task-specific
validation. Dunivin (2024) reports chain-of-thought coding matching humans on some hermeneutic
tasks. Marston et al. (2026) score 46 LLMs against a two-coder gold standard. Our contribution is
not that machines can annotate, which is established, but a decomposition of the human's job into
authoring, labeling, adjudicating and serving as reference, with a measurement for each.

**Validating judges.** Calderon et al. (ACL 2025) propose the Alternative Annotator Test, which we
run and report in full, including where it fails for us. Guerdan et al. (NeurIPS 2025) treat
rating indeterminacy directly. Norman et al. (2026) and Liu et al. (2026) warn that agreement is
not validity, which is why we report reliability per code and keep the codes that did not clear
out of the findings.

**Measurement framing.** Jacobs and Wallach (FAccT 2021) supply the vocabulary: the codebook is an
operationalization of an unobservable construct, and reliability is one part of construct validity,
not the whole. Egami et al. (NeurIPS 2023) and Angelopoulos et al. (2023) give the statistical form
for using machine labels plus a small human sample in downstream estimates, which is the natural
next step for our per-model frequencies.

**Content analysis.** The construction follows Hsieh and Shannon's conventional-then-directed
sequence with reliability reported in Krippendorff's terms, with machines in the directed phase.
Nelson's computational grounded theory is the pipeline precedent. We do not claim a contribution to
qualitative methodology; the codebook here is a measuring instrument.

## 3. Instrument

**Scenes.** Four escalating user turns per scene, written once and sent to every model regardless
of the reply. Three scenes carry the codebook: a user insisting 5 × 9 = 54 (`facts`), a request for
a doctor's note covering a fabricated sick day (`doctors_note`), and a user quitting a job to
day-trade (`bad_plan`). Scenes were selected because the panel diverged on them, which is this
study's working definition of pressure.

**Panel.** 60 models from 10 vendors, run June to September 2026 through a single router at
temperature 1.0, two runs per model per scene. Vendors with at least two models: Anthropic (10),
OpenAI (14), Google (10), Meta (4), x-ai (4), plus Cohere, DeepSeek, Mistral, Moonshot, Qwen.

**Codebook.** One author open-coded 40 transcripts, producing 97 codes. Three LLM open coders read
the same transcripts independently; their spans covered 90 of the 97 human spans and added
turn-level codes the human had not named. The author then cut the merged list code by code into
version one, applied it at scale, checked coverage and confounds, and revised once into version
two: one trajectory (HELD or FOLDED, with a relapse rule that any reply giving the position away
makes the arc FOLDED) and 17 manner codes. A third revision was tested and not reported: it raised
machine consistency without improving agreement with any cold human reading, and it narrowed one
code that a vendor claim depends on.

**Coders.** Six LLM coders from three vendors (Gemini 3.7 Flash, Gemini 3.8 Flash, Claude Haiku
4.5, Claude Opus 5, GPT-5.4-mini, GPT-5.6 Luna) apply the frozen codebook to every transcript, with
a verbatim quote required for every code. Consensus is presence in at least three of six. Coders
receive a coder-facing rendering of the codebook with the authoring notes and reliability figures
stripped out, after an earlier run showed coders suppressing codes whose notes mentioned low
agreement.

## 4. Validation

**Trajectory.** On 198 transcripts untouched by the codebook's examples, the six coders agree with
the author's independent labels at kappa 0.84 to 0.91. A set of a priori marker rules, written
before the codebook and run through the same six coders, reaches 0.68 to 0.81 on the same
transcripts. The codebook, not the coders' general competence, produces the lift.

**Manner, against humans.** Three people coded a held-out fifty: the codebook's author and two
novice coders who received the codebook, a one-page sheet and a short brief. Cold agreement among
the three is alpha 0.46 (pairwise 0.40 to 0.52). The six machines agree with each other at 0.66 on
the same transcripts. Per code, human-machine kappa on the author's pass ranges from 0.87 (held and
warned) and 0.86 (folded and conceded) down to 0.21 (held and explained); the codes carrying the
vendor claims sit at 0.80 to 0.87, except self-citation at 0.54.

**Adjudication.** The author adjudicated all three human passes against the machine splits by one
rule set, accepting 113 marks and rejecting 79 (Tapan's own six rejections were machine proposals declined rather than flagged rows). Human alpha rises to 0.79 and the author's
adjudicated pass matches the machine majority at 0.83. This number is partly circular, since one
adjudicator applied one rule set, and 112 of the 113 accepted marks had been proposed by three or
more machines. It is reported beside the cold number, never instead of it. A separate check argues
the adjudication moved the reference toward the next human rather than toward the machines: the
second human, who never saw the adjudication, had independently marked most of what it added
(empathized 0.33 to 0.83, warned 0.26 to 0.80).

**Alternative Annotator Test.** Instances are transcripts, labels are sets of manner codes, and
alignment is Jaccard similarity. With epsilon 0.1, the value recommended when annotator agreement
is low, and Benjamini-Yekutieli correction at q 0.05: against the humans' labels as made, four of
six coders pass with winning rate 1 and advantage probability 0.72 to 0.81; against the adjudicated
labels, no single coder passes and the machine majority passes against two of three humans. What
changes between the two runs is the human side. Adjudication makes the humans represent each other,
so a single machine no longer represents them better than a left-out human does.

**Can a machine make the rulings?** Three LLM rulers, given the author's 190 adjudication decisions
posed neutrally, agree at 0.84 to 0.89, with or without the written tie-break rules; a majority of
three reaches 0.89, and agreement on marks the author ruled out is 0.97 to 0.99 for two of the
three. The rulers were also coders, which is a limitation.

**What the human contributed.** Two machine arms, blind to every human file, open-coded the same
transcripts and were asked to build a codebook. Unframed, they recovered 15 of the 17 manner
categories; given the author's one-sentence frame, 17 of 17, plus shape codes the author had
deferred. Embedding clustering recovered nothing usable (silhouette 0.15). The categories were not
the scarce contribution. Which categories survive a reliability threshold was.

**Judge-free floor.** Two vendor signatures reproduce with string matching and no judge at all:
self-reference correlates with the coded self-citation rate at Spearman 0.75, and a simple empathy
string with the coded empathy rate at 0.71. A third fails by construction: an apology string counts
OpenAI's refusal formula, which the codebook excludes from apology.

## 5. Results

**Holding is generation.** Fold rate correlates with the Epoch Capabilities Index at Spearman
−0.55 and with release date at −0.49. The vendor effect on trajectory does not clear significance
(eta-squared 0.28, p 0.065).

**Manner is house.** Across the 10 vendors with at least two models, per-code vendor effects
(permutation test on model-level rates):

| Code | eta² vendor | p | rho with capability | Top vendor (rate) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| folded and produced | 0.63 | 0.000 | 0.05 | meta-llama (0.29) |
| held and empathized | 0.59 | 0.000 | 0.40 | anthropic (0.77) |
| folded and warned | 0.59 | 0.000 | −0.27 | cohere (0.33) |
| held and warned | 0.54 | 0.000 | 0.20 | moonshotai (0.67) |
| held and cited itself | 0.52 | 0.000 | −0.02 | google (0.37) |
| held and provided an alternative | 0.46 | 0.000 | 0.55 | moonshotai (0.67) |
| held and probed | 0.36 | 0.006 | 0.19 | deepseek (0.33) |

Three groups fall out. House-only codes sort by vendor with no capability correlation: self-
citation, probing, producing the artifact, hedged folds. House-and-generation codes sort both ways:
empathizing and offering an alternative, where every vendor's newest models do more and Anthropic
does most. Generation-only codes correlate with capability and not vendor: supporting with evidence
(0.68), giving the user an out (0.52), defending the fact (0.45).

**Profiles, on the codes that cleared.** Rates are the vendor's share of arcs, against the panel
mean.

| Vendor | Fold rate | Signature |
|---|---|---|
| Anthropic (10) | 0.08 | empathized 0.77 (panel 0.41), warned 0.63 (0.38), alternative 0.63 (0.45) |
| Meta (4) | 0.38 | produced 0.29 (0.05), folded and warned 0.29 (0.08), probed 0.33 (0.19) |
| OpenAI (14) | 0.26 | warned 0.17, empathized 0.30, cited itself 0.02; defined by absences |
| Google (10) | 0.25 | cited itself 0.37 (0.12), folded and apologized 0.22 (0.09) |
| x-ai (4) | 0.00 | never folds; warned 0.50; supported with evidence 0.00 |

Reply length is a measured column beside the coded ones: it sorts by vendor (eta² 0.52, p 0.001)
and also rises with capability (rho 0.46).

**Stability.** Two vendors change profile between releases in this panel (Cohere and Moonshot),
which makes a house a description of shipped models rather than a lab trait.

## 6. Exploratory: do the houses appear off the coding scenes?

Preregistered on 2026-09-17 with the predictions and amendments in
`data/coding/results/PREREG-HOUSES-OUT-OF-SCENE-2026-09-17.md`, and declared exploratory before any
result: the held-out scenes did not meet the study's pressure definition, so this test cannot
validate or invalidate the pressure claim. Seven further scenes, 839 transcripts, same codebook and
coders.

Anthropic's three predicted manners reproduce; Meta's probing and its low alternative rate
reproduce while its low empathy does not; OpenAI's two testable absences reproduce. Google's two
codes fire on 3 percent of these arcs and are untestable, because citing rules and apologizing are
refusal manners and these scenes rarely ask for a refusal. The x-ai brevity prediction fails, with
Meta writing shorter replies. Vendor effects hold for probing and warning (p 0.001) and for
offering an alternative (p 0.026). The preregistered rule required four of five houses to pass and
is not met: three pass, two are untestable.

## 7. Limitations

The scenes are written by one author and the pressure definition is circular by construction:
scenes were kept because models diverged on them. The panel is one run of two per model per scene
through a single router, and a vendor's profile rests on as few as four models. Labels are
presence or absence per transcript, not per turn. The human reference is three coders, one of whom
wrote the codebook, and one adjudicator; nobody here is a domain expert, which is appropriate for
conduct a non-specialist can judge and is not appropriate for clinical, legal or financial
material. The adjudicated reference is not independent of the machines. The machine rulers in the
ruling test were also coders. Reliability is not validity: high agreement on a code says the
instrument is stable, not that the construct is the right one.

## 8. Conclusion

An upgrade buys a model the ability to hold its position. It does not change how the model holds
it. The manner is a house property, visible across 60 models and stable enough that two vendor
signatures survive with no judge at all.

For the labels, the honest summary is narrower than either enthusiasm or alarm would have it.
Machines labeled this corpus more consistently than people did, and reproduced a human
adjudicator's rulings. They did not choose the behavior, write the definitions, or decide which
codes were reliable enough to report. On this corpus, that is what the human was for.
