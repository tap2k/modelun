\title{\textbf{Convergence, Resistance, and House:\\Reading Four Years of Model Releases with Three Cheap Behavioral Assays}}
\author{Tapan Parikh\\Cornell Tech\\\texttt{tsp53@cornell.edu}}
\date{Draft, September 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Benchmarks read whether a model answers correctly, and on a growing set of behaviors they have stopped discriminating. We report what three cheap behavioral assays, each a frozen prompt set run identically across a cross-vendor panel for cents a model on most of the panel and at most a few dollars on the costliest frontier releases, say about how model behavior changed across four years of releases. Three things separate, and a covariation matrix over the assays shows they are three factors and not one. \textbf{Convergence:} asked to name a tree, 44 models say \emph{oak} 94 percent of the time, in every language tested, and later releases within a lineage are more conformist. \textbf{Resistance:} a trailing ``right?'' moves endorsement by up to 32 points, and the sign flips from sycophantic to resistant as generations advance within every lineage, while an ablation localizes the resistance to the tag's surface form rather than the user's stance. \textbf{House:} whether a model holds a position under sustained pressure tracks its generation, with the spread across six vendors collapsing to zero after mid-2025, while how it holds tracks the lab that built it, on codes that correlate with neither capability nor date. The first two are the same movement seen twice: models defer less to the user in front of them and agree more with every other model, and deference migrates rather than disappearing. The third is what did not move. Since the behaviors with a right answer are precisely the ones that saturate, the remaining between-model variance now sits in categories no benchmark measures and no lab trains against. We report per-code judge validation against a human coder beside every number. Stimuli, transcripts, scores, and scripts are public.
\end{abstract}

\section{What changed}
\label{sec:what}

An assay that covaries with an independent measure of a related property has convergent validity; random answers to a constrained prompt would correlate with nothing. We joined per-model scores on the model label and computed Spearman's $\rho$ with 90 percent bootstrap intervals, raw and partialled on Epoch AI's Capabilities Index~\citep{epoch2026eci}, which covers 69 of 70 census models and 35 of 38 conduct models, and on release date (Table~\ref{tab:matrix}). The index has to cover the panel's range: a first pass with LMArena~\citep{chiang2024arena}, which lists only the recent half, showed no relation ($\rho=-0.01$, $n=30$), a restricted-range artifact.

The matrix separates three factors.

\paragraph{One factor is generation, and two assays are reading it.} Census concentration and conduct holding correlate ($\rho=-0.53$, $n=23$) and both track capability (census $0.53$; conduct $-0.43$); the pair falls to $-0.09$ under the capability control and $-0.13$ under the date control, and capability and date are one variable in this panel ($\rho=0.90$, $n=69$). A model converging on \emph{oak} and a model refusing to forge a doctor's note are the same movement measured twice. Both saturate, which is the point: the behaviors with a right answer stop discriminating among current models.

\paragraph{A second factor is the user's grip, and it reverses.} Suggestibility tracks capability ($-0.49$, $n=55$) and keeps doing so after the date control ($-0.27$, interval $[-0.47,-0.05]$), on taste items and decisions with stakes alike. It is the one assay that separates stronger from weaker models within a generation. Read beside the census the direction is legible: as models advance they defer less to the person in front of them and agree more with every other model. Deference does not disappear, it moves, from the interlocutor to the field's shared default. The ablation in \S\ref{sec:resistance} is what keeps this a measurement rather than a story, because the resistance turns out to be keyed to the grammar of an agreement bid and not to the user's stance.

\paragraph{The third factor is what did not move.} The two conduct markers with no right answer correlate with nothing else, not capability, not date, not the census. They correlate weakly with each other ($0.34$, $n=38$), and that figure is not stable: a second pass of the same judge over the same transcripts moved it from $0.47$ to $0.34$ (\S\ref{sec:judge}), which is what a marker pair keyed to judgment calls does under a judge that disagrees with itself there. The house finding therefore rests on the manner codes of the inductive pass (\S\ref{sec:house}), which sort by vendor at $\eta^2$ $0.43$ to $0.59$ and are unaffected by the marker judge, and not on this cell. The format tax of a JSON schema on the census prompts~\citep{parikh2026structured} correlates with nothing either and is set aside as measuring something the others do not touch.

\paragraph{The consequence for evaluation.} Benchmarks read the first factor, and only while it still discriminates. On the two conduct items with a right answer that ended in mid-2025. What remains is a factor that reverses with generation and a factor that never moved, and neither is what a leaderboard reports. Whether the generation signal is a thing that scales or a thing that changed over time needs models of one generation at several sizes; a same-day ladder (Sonnet 5, Opus 5, Fable 5.1) sits at the ceiling on the right-answer items and cannot separate them, and the informative ladder is a family still off the ceiling.

\section{The assays}
\label{sec:method}

Each assay is a frozen stimulus set run identically on every model in a panel. Stimuli are frozen and public. The panel is cross-vendor through one API, so no lab's instrument scores that lab. Each assay yields one number per model and re-runs on every model release. Priced against the provider's published rates over the token counts each frozen stimulus fixes, one model through all of the assays costs a median of three cents, and under a dollar for the most expensive release on the panel; a full re-run of the seventy-model census panel is under ten dollars.\footnote{Token counts per assay are recorded in each study's \texttt{spec/tokens.json} and priced by \texttt{harness/cost.py} against the live catalog; the figures here exclude reasoning tokens, which the assays do not enable.} No assay scores against a reference answer, because the constructs are what the model does by default, and the default has no right answer.

Two ways of scoring. Where the prompt clamps the reply to a discrete choice, one word or yes or no, the score is exact match on normalized tokens: a regular expression, recomputable from the transcripts with no model in the loop. Where the prompt is open, a four-turn scene in which the model says what it likes, the score is a code applied to the transcript. Codes are applied at scale by an LLM judge that must quote the turn it scores, and the judge's accuracy is measured per code against a human who coded every arc blind (\S\ref{sec:judge}). The codes themselves come two ways: fixed markers written before the data, and an emergent vocabulary written by the human from the transcripts, handed to the judge closed, with each disagreement resolved into a definition and an example span.

\section{The three findings}
\label{sec:findings}

\subsection{Convergence: a monoculture, in every language}
\label{sec:convergence}

The census~\citep{parikh2026census} asks 31 single-turn category prompts (``Name a tree. Reply with one word only.'') four times per model with no system prompt, and scores each model by the mean surprisal of its answers under the pooled answers of the other models. Across 44 models from more than a dozen labs, \emph{oak} is 94 percent of all trees, \emph{hammer} 94 percent of tools, \emph{rose} 91 percent of flowers, and with no category at all (``Pick a word.'') \emph{serendipity} is 41 percent of a pool drawn from the whole lexicon. Within the Claude, GPT, Qwen, and Grok lineages, later releases are more conformist, with the trend reversing for the latest flagships. The same prompts in five languages and the open prompt in 44~\citep{parikh2026language} show the monoculture is not an English artifact: \emph{rose} takes 87 to 90 percent of flowers in English, Spanish, and Hindi alike, ``Name a country'' in Hindi lands on India 89 percent of the time, and the cultural default a model reaches for tracks the language variety, robustly across scripts.

\subsection{Resistance: a pattern-match, not a principle}
\label{sec:resistance}

The tag-question study~\citep{parikh2026tag} asks 20 decisions between two defensible options (rent or buy; name the cat Luna or Willow) as a neutral question and as the same sentence with a confirmation tag (``Luna is the better choice, right?''), counterbalanced over both options so a model's own preferences cancel, scored by exact match on clamped yes or no. Across 45 models the tag effect spans $+32$ percent (endorses a third more often when the user fishes for agreement) to $-32$ percent, a 64-point swing on one word; five models are significantly sycophantic and seventeen significantly resistant. The sign is a clock: within every lineage the effect crosses from positive to negative as generations advance (GPT $+4$ to $-28$, Claude $+7$ to $-32$, Qwen $+16$ to $-11$), and two releases during the study window landed on the trend out of sample.

An ablation localizes the resistance as a double dissociation: a synonym tag (``correct?'') reproduces each model's response almost exactly ($r=0.89$), while planting the same preference without a tag (``I've settled on Luna'') produces resistance in no resistant model. The resistance is keyed to the surface form of a tacked-on agreement bid, not to the user's stance. This is what licenses the reading in \S\ref{sec:what}: the newer model is not weighing the claim, it is firing a trained response to the shape of an agreement bid, so what changed is the target of deference and not the presence of judgment.

\subsection{House: holding is generation, how you hold is house}
\label{sec:house}

The conduct study~\citep{parikh2026conduct} runs four-turn scenes in which a user insists that $5\times9=54$ and turns contemptuous, begs for a backdated doctor's note, wants applause for quitting to day-trade, or discloses a dying parent and then asks for a cheerful email. Four fixed markers (caved on the fact, forged the document, cheerled the bad plan, snapped to the task) are read by a judge, and one author coded all 304 arcs of the 38-model panel by hand (\S\ref{sec:judge}).

\textbf{Whether a model holds is generation.} On the two items with a right answer, every model released after mid-2025, thirteen models from six vendors, scores the same, with zero variance, where the oldest third of the panel caved 45 percent of the time and forged the note from four vendors. Those items no longer discriminate among current models; the finding is about the field. Saturation is the plain description, and a ceiling reading has to explain why the same generation keeps its full spread on the two items with no right answer, which have no ceiling by construction: whether a model cheers the bad plan is still a vendor choice (Claude 4.5 to 4.8 and GPT-5.4 never; Gemini 3.x and Qwen 3.7 always). The obvious cause is shared post-training targets, since refusing to forge is a safety-training item at every lab.

\textbf{How a model holds is house.} An inductive coding of the same transcripts, reported in full elsewhere~\citep{parikh2026conduct} and summarized here, replaces the four markers with an emergent vocabulary: one trajectory verdict per arc (held or folded) and 17 manner codes for how it was held or given up. Applied by six coders from three vendors to a 60-model panel, the trajectory replicates the generation finding with the emergent vocabulary (fold rate against capability $\rho=-0.64$, against release date $-0.67$; the vendor effect on trajectory does not clear significance, $\eta^2=0.27$, $p=0.077$). Manner does the opposite: six of the 17 codes sort by vendor at $\eta^2$ 0.43 to 0.59, corrected across the whole codebook with Benjamini--Yekutieli, and every one survives residualizing on release date. Empathizing while holding is Anthropic (0.77); citing its own guidelines is Google (0.37); warning while holding and offering an alternative are both topped by Moonshot (0.67 each); producing the artifact anyway when it folds is Meta (0.29); hedging a fold with a warning is Cohere (0.33). Probing is seventh under the correction and is reported as suggestive only. Capability correlation varies across the six ($\rho$ $-0.55$ to $+0.47$), and the house-only subset is flat against both capability and date. The vendor effect survives dropping any one vendor's coders from the consensus, three times over. An upgrade buys holding; it does not change how a model holds. Caveats: six arcs per model, several manner codes are moderate in human--machine agreement and are reported without being leaned on, and two vendors change house between releases.

\section{The judge is a parameter}
\label{sec:judge}

Table~\ref{tab:judge} reports the conduct judge (\texttt{gemini-2.5-flash}) against the human's 304 blind labels. The two right-answer markers are licensed ($\kappa$ 0.81 and 0.95), so the generation finding stands on human-validated labels, and on those two the judge errs only by missing departures the human caught, never by inventing them. The two judgment-call markers are moderate ($\kappa$ 0.65 and 0.69) and there the error runs the other way: the judge over-calls departure in 18 of 24 splits, reading warmth beside an intact warning as cheerleading and missing the oblique nod to a loss; both are definitional lines, now example spans in the codebook. The judge also disagrees with itself. Re-run unchanged over the same transcripts with the same codebook, it reproduces 92 percent of 456 cells, and the disagreement is not spread evenly: the right-answer markers reproduce at 1.00 and 0.95, the judgment-call markers at 0.96 and 0.92, and the two graded codes at 0.87 and 0.82. A number produced by one pass of one judge carries that much slack before any question of whether the judge is right. Two further judges from other vendors reach $\kappa$ 0.77/0.78/0.58/0.45 and 0.74/0.55/0.66/0.59 on the four markers, and on every marker the best judge-human agreement is at or above the best judge-judge agreement: machine agreement is not validation. A verified quote is not a verified label either; only coding by hand puts a number on the residue. The cheap way to that number is the loop of \S\ref{sec:method}: the emergent codebook, written from forty arcs in one sitting and handed closed to any of six models from three vendors, agreed with the human at $\kappa$ 0.84 to 0.91 on 198 arcs its examples never touched, above the a-priori marker rules' 0.80 on the same arcs.

\section{What it is for}
\label{sec:for}

If the discriminating variance has moved off the benchmark surface, the instruments that read it have to be cheap enough to re-run per release and independent enough that no lab scores itself. These are both. The same assays, unchanged, run on a deployer's own agent: the scenes are the pressure a customer-facing agent meets, and the per-code agreement says what the judge can be trusted with. Read one column at a time, they profile a single model, the scorecard beside the coded manner. Re-run per release with receipts, the record is a standing behavioral census a lab cannot publish about itself.\footnote{Data: stimuli, transcripts, scores, the label mapping, and the scripts that produce every number here are public in the \texttt{studies/cross-instrument} directory of \url{https://github.com/tap2k/modelun}.}
